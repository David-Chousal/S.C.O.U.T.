# External Stakeholder Interview Summary

> **Summary** — Findings from Summer 2026 interviews with three NOAA coral reef researchers, and the resulting recommended direction for SCOUT.
>
> **Source document** — `Stakeholder Meetings.docx`

---

**Interviewees** — Dr. Andy Shantz · Dr. Hannah Barkley · Thomas Oliver (NOAA) · **Period** — Summer 2026

---

## Purpose

Over the course of Summer 2026, several discussions were held with NOAA researchers and coral reef monitoring experts to better understand current monitoring needs, existing gaps, operational challenges, and opportunities for SCOUT. The purpose of these meetings was to evaluate the potential scientific value, technical feasibility, and practical applications of a low-cost reef monitoring platform.

Interviewees included:

- Dr. Andy Shantz

- Dr. Hannah Barkley

- Thomas Oliver

The following sections summarize the major findings from each discussion.

## 1. Dr. Andy Shantz

### Major Themes

- Reef acoustics and biodiversity monitoring

- Turbidity and sedimentation

- Water quality impacts on coral reefs

- Biofouling challenges

- Environmental sensor priorities

### Acoustics and Reef Health

Acoustic monitoring was identified as a promising approach for reef monitoring. Certain organisms create identifiable sound signatures that may indicate reef health and ecosystem function.

Examples discussed include:

- Parrotfish scraping sounds as indicators of herbivore populations.

- Fish acoustic signatures that may reflect species composition.

- Reef soundscapes as indicators of biodiversity.

Acoustics may also detect:

- Fishing activity.

- Boat traffic.

- Human disturbance.

Although interpretation remains challenging, acoustic data could provide a unique ecological dataset.

### Turbidity and Sedimentation

Turbidity was identified as a highly valuable measurement.

Potential applications include:

- Sediment runoff monitoring.

- Watershed impacts.

- Water quality assessment.

- Coral stress monitoring.

Multiple turbidity measurements at different depths may allow the observation of turbidity stratification throughout the water column.

### Light and Water Clarity

Combining above-water light measurements with underwater turbidity measurements could estimate how much light reaches the reef.

This information is important because coral growth depends heavily on available light.

PAR measurements were discussed but considered lower priority.

### Temperature

Temperature was consistently identified as one of the most valuable measurements.

Advantages include:

- Low cost.

- Easy implementation.

- Extensive historical datasets.

- Relevance to nearly all reef studies.

### Nutrients and Dissolved Oxygen

Nutrients and dissolved oxygen were considered scientifically interesting but difficult.

Challenges include:

- Sensor complexity.

- Calibration requirements.

- Biofouling.

- Increased cost.

These measurements may be better suited for future versions of SCOUT.

### Biofouling

Biofouling was identified as a major concern.

Impacts include:

- Reduced sensor accuracy.

- Optical sensor degradation.

- Turbidity sensor interference.

Potential mitigation methods include:

- Mechanical wipers.

- Reef-safe antifouling methods.

- Improved sensor placement.

### Key Takeaways

- Temperature remains essential.

- Turbidity is highly valuable.

- Acoustics provide unique information.

- Biofouling must be addressed.

- Combining measurements provides greater value than individual sensors.

## 2. Dr. Hannah Barkley

### Major Themes

- Temperature monitoring.

- Nearshore monitoring gaps.

- Data accessibility.

- Long-term deployments.

- Maintenance limitations.

### In Situ Temperature

Dr. Barkley emphasized the importance of in situ temperature measurements.

While satellite products provide surface temperatures, subsurface reef environments remain under-monitored.

Applications include:

- Marine heatwave detection.

- El Niño monitoring.

- Coral bleaching studies.

- Validation of remote sensing products.

### Satellite Limitations

Nearshore environments often perform poorly in satellite products.

Challenges include:

- Shallow water.

- Bottom reflectance.

- Coastal complexity.

This creates an opportunity for local sensor measurements.

### Turbidity and Sedimentation

Turbidity remains an important indicator of:

- Sedimentation.

- Runoff.

- Light reduction.

- Water quality.

Temperature and turbidity together may help explain coral stress events.

### Chlorophyll and Nutrients

Chlorophyll may provide a useful proxy for productivity and nutrient availability.

However:

- Satellite chlorophyll performs poorly near shore.

- Direct measurements add complexity.

Nutrient measurements remain difficult.

### pH and Ocean Acidification

pH and carbonate chemistry provide information about:

- Ocean acidification.

- Reef calcification.

- Reef growth.

However:

- Sensors are expensive.

- Maintenance is difficult.

- Reliability remains challenging.

### Data Accessibility

One major issue discussed was delayed data access.

Some existing monitoring programs only retrieve data every several years.

This limits:

- Rapid response.

- Management decisions.

- Event detection.

SCOUT’s communication capabilities may significantly improve data accessibility.

### Deployment and Servicing

Typical servicing intervals discussed:

- Monthly servicing is considered frequent.

- Six-month intervals are common.

- Remote sites may only be serviced every several years.

Low maintenance is therefore critical.

### Cost Considerations

Typical monitoring systems range from:

- Hundreds of dollars for individual loggers.

- Several thousand dollars for moderate systems.

- Tens of thousands of dollars for advanced buoys.

A target cost below $5,000 was discussed.

### Key Takeaways

- In situ temperature remains extremely valuable.

- Data accessibility is a major problem.

- Nearshore environments are poorly monitored.

- Low-maintenance systems are needed.

- Cost and service intervals strongly influence deployment success.

## 3. Thomas Oliver

### Major Themes

- Water quality.

- Cost and scalability.

- Satellite validatio

- Stakeholder needs.

- Deployment logistics.

### Point Measurements Versus Environmental Understanding

Temperature sensors provide valuable data, but isolated measurements do not always explain entire environmental conditions.

Tom emphasized integrating sensor data into:

- Models.

- Satellite products.

- Larger environmental frameworks.

### Thermistor Chains

Multiple temperature measurements throughout the water column can identify:

- Thermoclines.

- Temperature stratification.

- Vertical environmental structure.

### Deployment and Anchoring

A major point emphasized was that deployment systems are as important as sensors.

Critical factors include:

- Anchors.

- Moorings.

- Recovery systems.

- Long-term stability.

Sensor selection alone does not determine success.

### Acoustics

Acoustics were viewed more cautiously.

Advantages:

- Useful information.

- Reef activity measurements.

- Biodiversity insights.

Limitations:

- Many fish are quiet.

- Acoustic data are biased.

- Data processing is difficult.

Acoustics remain valuable but require substantial analysis.

### Water Quality

Tom strongly emphasized water quality monitoring.

Applications include:

- Sediment monitoring.

- Brown water events.

- Runoff.

- Coastal water quality.

Turbidity was considered especially valuable because it is:

- Inexpensive.

- Reliable.

- Easy to interpret.

### Satellite Validation

Satellite products often struggle in shallow coastal environments.

Problems include:

- Bottom reflectance.

- Shallow water interference.

- Coastal complexity.

SCOUT may provide valuable ground-truth measurements.

### Combining Satellite and In Situ Data

One of Tom’s strongest recommendations was to integrate SCOUT data with satellite products.

Potential outputs include:

- Water quality maps.

- Corrected satellite products.

- Spatial visualizations.

### Stakeholders

Water quality monitoring serves more stakeholders than coral reef monitoring alone.

Potential users include:

- Coastal managers.

- Tourism agencies.

- Public health groups.

- Environmental organizations.

- Municipal agencies.

This may improve funding opportunities.

### Key Takeaways

- Water quality is a strong problem to solve.

- Turbidity has broad applications.

- Cost matters.

- Satellite validation is valuable.

- Stakeholder diversity increases impact.

## Overall Findings

Several consistent themes emerged across all three interviews.

### Temperature

All three experts strongly supported temperature monitoring.

Benefits:

- Low cost.

- High value.

- Long historical records.

- Broad applications.

### Turbidity

Turbidity received perhaps the strongest support overall.

Applications include:

- Sediment monitoring.

- Water quality.

- Runoff.

- Light reduction.

- Brown water events.

### Acoustics

Acoustics generated interest but also caution.

Benefits:

- Biodiversity.

- Fish activity.

- Human activity.

Challenges:

- Data interpretation.

- Quiet species.

- Large datasets.

### Chlorophyll

Chlorophyll may provide:

- Productivity information.

- Water quality indicators.

- Satellite validation.

However, implementation complexity remains uncertain.

### Carbonate Chemistry

All interviewees agreed that pH and carbonate measurements are scientifically valuable but currently difficult.

Challenges include:

- Cost.

- Reliability.

- Maintenance.

### Data Accessibility

Current monitoring programs often suffer from delayed access to data.

SCOUT could provide:

- Near real-time observations.

- Improved accessibility.

- Faster responses.

### Satellite Validation

Nearshore environments are poorly represented by existing satellite products.

SCOUT measurements may:

- Validate satellite observations.

- Improve models.

- Fill important data gaps.

## Recommended SCOUT Direction

### Highest Priority

- Temperature.

- Turbidity.

- Water quality monitoring.

- Low-cost deployment.

- Data accessibility.

### Medium Priority

- Acoustics.

- Chlorophyll.

- Multi-depth temperature.

### Lower Priority

- pH.

- Carbonate chemistry.

- Nutrient sensors.

- Dissolved oxygen.

## Proposed Problem Statement

Rather than solely describing SCOUT as a coral reef monitoring buoy, the interviews suggest a broader framing:

“SCOUT is a low-cost nearshore water quality and reef monitoring platform that combines in situ environmental measurements with remote sensing products to improve coastal monitoring and data accessibility.”

## Final Conclusion

Across all interviews, the strongest consensus was that low-cost, accessible measurements of temperature and water quality fill a significant monitoring gap.

SCOUT may have its greatest impact not by replacing existing monitoring systems, but by providing affordable, scalable, and accessible environmental measurements that complement existing observations and improve coastal monitoring.
